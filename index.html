01

Atomic foundations & basic terms

Atomic number, mass number, particles, isotopes and related terms

An atom has a tiny central nucleus containing protons and usually neutrons, with electrons occupying the surrounding region. In a neutral atom the positive and negative charges balance.

ParticleRelative chargeApprox. relative massLocation
Electron (e⁻)−11/1836Outside nucleus
Proton (p⁺)+11 (about 1 u)Nucleus
Neutron (n⁰)01 (slightly heavier than a proton)Nucleus
Atomic-number bookkeeping
Z = number of protons
A = number of protons + number of neutrons
N = A − Z
Neutral atom: electrons = Z

For an ion, calculate electron count from the charge. A cation has lost electrons; an anion has gained electrons. For example, Na⁺ has 10 electrons, while O²⁻ has 10 electrons.

Isotopes

Same atomic number Z, different mass number A. Hydrogen isotopes: protium ¹H, deuterium ²H and tritium ³H. The lecture notes state that about 99.985% of naturally occurring hydrogen atoms are protium.

Isobars

Same mass number A, different atomic number Z. Example: ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca.

Isotones

Same number of neutrons, different Z and usually different A. Example: ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N each have 8 neutrons.

Isoelectronic species

Same number of electrons. Example: O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each have 10 electrons.

Important line: the number of protons identifies the element. Isotopes differ in neutrons, not in proton count.
Common trap: do not calculate neutrons from Z alone. Use N = A − Z; then adjust the electron count for the ionic charge.
02

Discovery of particles & atomic models

Cathode rays, positive rays, Thomson, Rutherford and model limitations

Cathode rays and the electron

Cathode rays are produced in a discharge tube at low gas pressure and high potential difference. They start at the cathode and move towards the anode.

  • They are not directly visible, but their path can be detected with fluorescent or phosphorescent material.
  • In the absence of electric or magnetic fields, they travel in straight lines.
  • In electric and magnetic fields they behave as negatively charged particles.
  • They can cause fluorescence, heat objects, ionise gases and produce X-rays when they strike a solid target.
  • Their nature is independent of the gas in the tube and the electrode material: cathode rays are streams of electrons.
Electron charge-to-mass ratio: e/m ≈ −1.76 × 10⁸ C g⁻¹
Magnitude in SI units: 1.76 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹. The negative sign refers to electron charge. Thomson measured e/m using perpendicular electric and magnetic fields.

Millikan’s oil-drop experiment

Millikan measured the elementary charge by observing charged oil droplets between electric plates. The magnitude of a droplet’s charge was found to be an integral multiple of e.

Charge magnitude: e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
Electron mass: mₑ = |e| / |e/m| ≈ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg

Proton and neutron

  • Goldstein observed positive / anode rays using a perforated cathode. Their charge-to-mass ratio depends on the gas. The lightest positive ion obtained from hydrogen is the proton.
  • Proton charge = +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; proton mass ≈ 1.672 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • Neutrons are electrically neutral and, with protons, reside in the nucleus.

Canal / anode rays

Canal rays (positive rays) travel in the direction opposite to cathode rays through perforations in the cathode. They are positively charged; unlike cathode rays, their properties depend on the gas present because the ions formed can differ.

Thomson model

Thomson proposed that positive charge was spread throughout the atom with electrons embedded in it, often compared with seeds in a positive sphere. The model explained overall neutrality but did not explain the alpha-particle scattering results.

Rutherford’s alpha-particle scattering experiment

A beam of alpha particles (helium nuclei) was directed at a very thin metal foil.

Observations

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through.
  • Some were deflected through small angles.
  • A very small number were deflected through large angles or came back.

Conclusions

  • Most of an atom is empty space.
  • Positive charge and most atomic mass are concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus.
  • The nucleus is at the centre; electrons occupy the surrounding region.
High-yield line from the lecture set: the scattering beam was made of alpha particles / helium nuclei, not electrons or ordinary helium atoms.

Why Rutherford’s model was incomplete

According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle should radiate energy. An electron in a classical orbit should therefore lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. The model also did not account for the discrete line spectrum of hydrogen.

Model timeline

  1. Thomson: embedded electrons in a diffuse positive sphere.
  2. Rutherford: tiny, positive nucleus; atom mostly empty space.
  3. Bohr: only certain stationary orbits / energy levels are allowed.
  4. Quantum-mechanical model: electrons are described by wavefunctions and orbitals, not fixed classical trajectories.

X-rays and radioactivity (quick facts)

  • Roentgen discovered X-rays emitted when cathode rays strike a target. X-rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation (the lecture notes give a wavelength near 0.1 nm), penetrate matter strongly and are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
  • Moseley’s study of characteristic X-rays established a relationship between atomic number and X-ray frequency (often written √ν ∝ Z − b for a series, where b is a screening constant).
  • Radioactive substances emit α, β and γ radiations. α particles are doubly charged helium nuclei; β radiation in the lecture examples is negatively charged and deflected towards the positive plate; γ rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation.
  • Penetrating power increases roughly α < β < γ; ionising power is generally strongest for α. γ rays are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
Approximate scale: atomic radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m; nuclear radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Nuclear radius: R = R₀ A1/3, R₀ ≈ 1.3 fm.
The nucleus occupies a tiny fraction of the atom’s volume (roughly 10⁻¹⁵ by order of magnitude).
Common trap: cathode-ray properties do not depend on the gas or cathode material; canal-ray composition does depend on the gas. Millikan measured charge, while Thomson measured e/m.
03

Electromagnetic radiation & spectrum

Wavelength, frequency, wavenumber, units and spectrum terms

c = λν
ν = c/λ   ·   λ = c/ν
Wavenumber: ṽ = 1/λ (λ in cm gives ṽ in cm⁻¹)
c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Frequency ν is measured in hertz (Hz = s⁻¹); λ is wavelength.

Maxwell described electromagnetic radiation as changing electric and magnetic fields that propagate as waves. These fields are mutually perpendicular and perpendicular to the direction of propagation. EM waves travel through vacuum without needing a material medium.

Frequency ν = 1/T; T is the time period of one cycle.
Wave speed = wavelength × frequency = λν.
For a sinusoidal wave, intensity is proportional to the square of its amplitude.
If wavelength decreases, frequency and photon energy increase. Frequency is the number of wave cycles passing a point per second; Hz is named after Heinrich Hertz. An accelerating electric charge emits electromagnetic radiation.

Electromagnetic spectrum

Order, low to high frequencyRelative wavelengthExamples / notes
Radio wavesLongestBroadcasting; very low frequency
Microwaves↓Radar / microwave region
Infrared (IR)↓Thermal radiation
Visible↓Red has longer wavelength than violet
Ultraviolet (UV)↓Higher frequency than visible light
X-rays↓Can be produced when cathode rays strike solids
Gamma raysShortestVery high frequency / photon energy

Spectra: continuous vs discontinuous

A spectrum is a range or series of wavelengths. A continuous spectrum contains an uninterrupted spread of wavelengths / colours. A discontinuous (line) spectrum has gaps: only certain wavelengths appear. Atomic line spectra are evidence for quantised energy levels.

Black-body radiation

An ideal black body absorbs and emits radiation over all frequencies; a real material such as carbon black can approximate it. At thermal equilibrium, it emits as much energy per unit area as it absorbs over the corresponding time. The intensity distribution depends on temperature.

  • When a body is heated, it may glow red first, then white and at higher temperature bluish.
  • As temperature rises, the intensity peak shifts towards shorter wavelength / higher frequency.
Common trap: do not confuse intensity with photon energy. For monochromatic light, photon energy is controlled by frequency; intensity mainly controls how many photons arrive per unit time.
04

Planck’s quantum theory & photon energy

Quantised exchange of energy and unit conversions

Classical theory could not satisfactorily explain the observed black-body spectrum. Planck proposed that energy is emitted or absorbed in discrete packets (quanta), rather than in any arbitrary continuous amount.

E = hν for one photon / quantum
E = nhν for n allowed quanta (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
E = hc/λ
Planck constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Very useful shortcut: E (eV) ≈ 1240 / λ (nm)
Equivalently, E(eV) ≈ 12400 / λ(Å). Convert the wavelength to the matching unit before substituting.

Quick worked examples

λ = 500 nm

E = 1240/500 = 2.48 eV per photon.

λ = 400 nm

E = 1240/400 = 3.10 eV per photon.

For a collection of photons, total radiation energy is the number of photons times the energy per photon. Use Etotal = Nhν when N photons have the same frequency.

Conservation of energy in radiation transitions

When radiation is absorbed or emitted, energy balance must hold. The absorbed photon energy can be converted into other forms; it is not created or destroyed. In an atomic transition, the photon energy equals the energy difference between the two permitted states.

1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m   ·   1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m
1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz   ·   1 kHz = 10³ Hz
Common trap: E ∝ ν but E ∝ 1/λ. Doubling wavelength halves photon energy; doubling frequency doubles photon energy.
05

Photoelectric effect

Experimental observations, threshold frequency and Einstein’s equation

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a metal surface when suitable incident radiation strikes it. Hertz observed this behaviour; Einstein explained it using photons.

Important experimental observations

  • Emission begins essentially without measurable time lag when the frequency is suitable.
  • The number of emitted photoelectrons / photoelectric current increases with intensity, provided the incident frequency is above threshold.
  • Each metal has a characteristic threshold frequency ν₀. Below ν₀, photoemission does not occur, however high the intensity.
  • The maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons increases with frequency, not with intensity alone.
Einstein photoelectric equation
hν = Φ + Kmax
Kmax = hν − hν₀ = h(ν − ν₀)
Φ = hν₀ = hc/λ₀
Kmax = ½m(vmax)² = eVs
Φ = work function; ν₀ = threshold frequency; λ₀ = threshold wavelength; Vₛ = stopping potential.
If you increase…What changes?What does not change (ideal monochromatic case)?
Intensity at ν > ν₀More photons per second; more emitted electrons / larger currentKmax remains set by ν
Frequency νKmax and stopping potential increasePhoton count does not have to increase
Frequency below ν₀No photoelectric emissionIncreasing intensity alone cannot overcome the threshold
Photons transfer energy in packets. One absorbed photon can eject an electron only if its energy meets or exceeds the metal’s work function; the excess appears as kinetic energy.

Worked example: wavelength 400 nm, work function 2.13 eV

Photon energy = 1240/400 = 3.10 eV
Kmax = 3.10 − 2.13 = 0.97 eV
Convert K to joules before using v = √(2K/m).

Threshold wavelength

At threshold, the emitted electron has Kmax = 0. Therefore Φ = hν₀ = hc/λ₀. Wavelengths shorter than λ₀ have enough photon energy; longer wavelengths do not.

Useful graph ideas

  • Kmax vs frequency is a straight line with slope h and a frequency-axis intercept ν₀.
  • Photoelectric current increases with intensity at suitable frequency.
  • Stopping potential is proportional to maximum kinetic energy: eVs = Kmax.
Common trap: “more intense light” does not mean “more energetic photons”. Frequency sets the energy of each photon; intensity sets the photon flux.
06

Bohr model: postulates & formulae

Permitted orbits, angular momentum, radius, velocity and energy

Bohr combined Planck’s quantum idea with a nuclear atom. In the supplied lecture notes, the model is explicitly limited to one-electron (hydrogen-like) species such as H, He⁺ and Li²⁺. It should not be applied to neutral He or other multi-electron species.

Postulates

  1. The atom has a tiny central nucleus containing its protons and neutrons; electrons move around it.
  2. The electrostatic attraction between nucleus and electron supplies the centripetal force for a circular orbit.
  3. Only certain orbits are permitted. In these stationary states, the electron’s energy remains constant and it does not radiate energy continuously.
  4. Angular momentum is quantised: only integral multiples of h/(2π) are allowed.
  5. Energy is absorbed when an electron jumps from an inner to an outer orbit; energy is emitted when it moves from an outer to an inner orbit.
Electrostatic force = centripetal force
kZe²/r² = mv²/r
mvr = nh/(2π),   n = 1, 2, 3, …
Z is nuclear charge number; k = 1/(4πε₀). Use SI units consistently when calculating. The permitted orbit contains n de Broglie wavelengths: 2πr = nλ.

Hydrogen-like atom / ion formulae

Orbit radius: rₙ = a₀ n²/Z = 0.529 n²/Z Å
Electron speed: vₙ = 2.18 × 10⁶ (Z/n) m s⁻¹
Total energy: Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² eV
In joules: Eₙ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ Z²/n² J
Ionisation energy from level n: 13.6 Z²/n² eV

How radius changes

At fixed Z, r ∝ n². At fixed n, r ∝ 1/Z. A larger nuclear charge pulls the electron closer.

How energy changes

E is negative for a bound state. As n increases, E becomes less negative and approaches 0 from below. Ionisation means taking the electron to n = ∞.

Energy, kinetic energy and potential energy

E = KE + PE
For a Bohr Coulomb orbit: KE = −E = |E|
PE = 2E = −2KE
Thus total energy is negative; kinetic energy is positive; potential energy is negative.

Transition energy

Ephoton = |Ef − Ei|
For emission (nᵢ > nf): Ephoton = 13.6 Z²(1/nf² − 1/nᵢ²) eV
Quick identification: H, He⁺, Li²⁺ and Be³⁺ are one-electron species. He, Li⁺, Be²⁺ and C⁴⁺ are not hydrogen-like because they have more than one electron.
Common trap: first excited state means n = 2; second excited means n = 3; third excited means n = 4. Ground state is n = 1.

Fast ratios to remember

  • For the same ion: r₂/r₁ = 4; r₃/r₁ = 9; r₄/r₁ = 16.
  • For the same n: r ∝ 1/Z, v ∝ Z, and |E| ∝ Z².
  • For the same Z: v ∝ 1/n and orbital time period T ∝ n³/Z².
  • For hydrogen: first excited energy E₂ = −3.40 eV; second excited E₃ = −1.51 eV; third excited E₄ = −0.85 eV.
  • Ground-state H and first-excited He⁺ both have E = −13.6 eV. Compare the actual n and Z before calculating ratios.

Why the Bohr model fails

  1. It does not explain spectra of multi-electron atoms / ions.
  2. It does not derive why angular momentum must equal nh/(2π); it assumes this rule.
  3. It does not satisfactorily explain fine structure of spectral lines.
  4. It does not explain splitting in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect) or electric field (Stark effect).
  5. It mixes classical mechanics and quantum ideas without a complete theory.

Hydrogen atomic spectrum

Line spectra, Rydberg equation, spectral series and line counting

When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower level, a photon is emitted with energy equal to the level difference. Absorption occurs for the reverse transition. Because only certain energy levels are allowed, the spectrum contains discrete lines.

Rydberg equation
1/λ = RZ² (1/nf² − 1/ni²), with nᵢ > nf
R ≈ 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ = 109677 cm⁻¹
Use Z = 1 for hydrogen; use the nuclear charge number for a hydrogen-like ion. The lower level is nₑ / nf, and the higher level is nᵢ.
SeriesFinal level nfInitial levelsRegion
Lyman12, 3, 4, …Ultraviolet
Balmer23, 4, 5, …Mainly visible
Paschen34, 5, 6, …Infrared
Brackett45, 6, 7, …Infrared
Pfund56, 7, 8, …Infrared
Humphreys67, 8, 9, …Far infrared

Longest wavelength in a series

Smallest energy gap within that series: first line, nᵢ = nf + 1. It has the lowest photon energy.

Shortest wavelength in a series

Series limit: nᵢ → ∞. It has the largest energy gap / highest photon energy and the shortest wavelength.

How many spectral lines?

If an electron initially at level n can make all downward transitions among levels 1 to n, the maximum number of distinct lines is N = n(n − 1)/2.
Example: from n = 4, N = 4 × 3 / 2 = 6 possible transitions. For a specified series ending at nf, count only transitions whose final level is that series level.
The series name is determined by the final energy level, not by the initial level. For example, every transition ending at n = 2 belongs to the Balmer series.

Hydrogen energy-level reminders

  • Ground state n = 1: −13.6 eV.
  • First excited n = 2: −3.40 eV.
  • Second excited n = 3: −1.51 eV.
  • Third excited n = 4: −0.85 eV.
  • Ionisation limit n = ∞: 0 eV.

For a transition in the same species, increasing the energy difference lowers λ and raises frequency. Compare the gaps, not just the level numbers.

Common trap: do not reverse the subtraction in the Rydberg expression. Put the smaller (final) principal quantum number first inside the reciprocal-square difference.
08

de Broglie hypothesis

Wave nature of matter and useful wavelength shortcuts

de Broglie proposed that moving particles also have wave nature. The wavelength associated with a particle is called its matter-wave or de Broglie wavelength.

λ = h/p = h/(mv) (non-relativistic particle)
Since K = p²/(2m):   λ = h/√(2mK)
For an electron accelerated from rest through potential V: λ(Å) ≈ 12.27/√V
V is in volts; this shortcut assumes a non-relativistic electron.

How the wavelength changes

  • At fixed mass, increasing speed / momentum decreases λ.
  • At fixed kinetic energy, λ ∝ 1/√m. The more massive particle has the shorter de Broglie wavelength.
  • At fixed particle mass, λ ∝ 1/√K. If kinetic energy becomes four times larger, wavelength becomes half.
  • For a particle accelerated through voltage V from rest, λ ∝ 1/√V.

Connection with Bohr’s allowed orbits

Only a standing matter wave fits an integer number of wavelengths around the orbit:

2πr = nλ, so λ = 2πr/n
Among electron, proton and neutron at the same kinetic energy, the most massive particle has the shortest wavelength. A macroscopic object such as a tennis ball has an unimaginably tiny wavelength.

Numerical checks

Tennis ball

m = 0.060 kg, v = 10 m s⁻¹. λ = h/(mv) ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴/0.60 ≈ 1.10×10⁻³³ m.

Voltage ratio

If an electron is accelerated through 50 V and 200 V, λ₅₀/λ₂₀₀ = √(200/50) = 2:1.

Common trap: at the same kinetic energy, wavelength scales with 1/√mass, not 1/m. Do not use λ = h/mv unless speed is known.
09

Heisenberg uncertainty principle

Position–momentum uncertainty and numerical estimates

It is impossible to determine the exact position and exact momentum of a microscopic particle simultaneously with unlimited precision. This is a fundamental quantum limitation, not merely a poor instrument.

Δx · Δp ≥ h/(4π)
For a non-relativistic particle of mass m: Δx · Δv ≥ h/(4πm)
Δx = position uncertainty; Δp = momentum uncertainty; Δv = velocity uncertainty.
  • Making the position uncertainty smaller forces the minimum momentum / velocity uncertainty to become larger.
  • For electrons, the effect is significant at atomic scales.
  • For macroscopic bodies, the uncertainty is ordinarily far too small to observe.

Worked-style example from the lecture theme

If an electron’s position is located within 0.1 Å, then Δx = 0.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 1 × 10⁻¹¹ m.

Δvmin = h/(4πmₑΔx)
≈ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π × 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ × 10⁻¹¹)
≈ 5.79 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
Use the inequality to find the minimum uncertainty. Convert Å to metres and use the mass of the particle given in the question.
Common trap: position uncertainty and velocity uncertainty have different units, so they cannot literally be compared as equal quantities without a defined numerical convention.
10

Quantum-mechanical model & quantum numbers

Orbitals, probability, permitted quantum-number sets

The quantum-mechanical model describes an electron using a wavefunction ψ. The quantity |ψ|² gives probability density. An orbital is a quantum state / region described probabilistically; it is not a fixed circular path.

Quantum numberSymbolAllowed valuesMeaning
Principaln1, 2, 3, …Shell, size and main energy scale
Azimuthal / angular momentuml0 to n−1Subshell / orbital shape
Magneticmₗ−l, …, 0, …, +lOrbital orientation; 2l+1 values
Spinmₛ+½ or −½Electron spin state

Subshell letters

l = 0 → s   ·   l = 1 → p   ·   l = 2 → d   ·   l = 3 → f
Number of orbitals in a subshell = 2l + 1
Maximum electrons in a subshell = 2(2l + 1) = 4l + 2

Allowed and disallowed combinations

  • For n = 1: l = 0 only, so only 1s is permitted.
  • For n = 2: l = 0 or 1, so 2s and 2p are permitted; 2d is not permitted.
  • For n = 3: l = 0, 1 or 2, so 3s, 3p and 3d are permitted; 3f is not permitted.
  • For l = 2, mₗ may be −2, −1, 0, +1 or +2: five orbitals.
  • Each electron has mₛ = +½ or −½.
Check validity in order: first n ≥ 1; next 0 ≤ l ≤ n−1; next mₗ must be an integer between −l and +l; finally mₛ = ±½.

Schrödinger equation & what the wavefunction means

The lecture notes introduce the Schrödinger equation as the equation whose allowed solutions give the wavefunctions and energies of an electron. In the time-independent form:

−(h²/8π²m) ∇²ψ + Vψ = Eψ
or, in operator notation, Ĥψ = Eψ
ψ is the wavefunction; V is potential energy; E is an allowed energy eigenvalue. The full atomic solution is commonly separated into radial and angular parts, ψ = R(r)Y(θ,φ).
ψ itself is not directly an observable. |ψ|² is probability density; probability in a small volume element is |ψ|² dτ. For a normalised wavefunction, the total probability over all space is 1.

Orbital and spin angular momentum

Orbital angular-momentum magnitude: L = √[l(l+1)] ħ = (h/2π)√[l(l+1)]
For electron spin s = 1/2: S = √[s(s+1)] ħ = (√3/2)ħ
s, p, d and f have l = 0, 1, 2 and 3 respectively. The spin projection quantum number is mₛ = ±1/2.

The magnetic quantum number mₗ gives the allowed spatial orientations of the orbital angular momentum; there are 2l+1 allowed orientations for a given l.

Spherical coordinates & wavefunctions

Because a nucleus-centred atom has spherical symmetry, the lecture notes use spherical coordinates as well as Cartesian coordinates:

x = r sinθ cosφ
y = r sinθ sinφ
z = r cosθ

Reference hydrogenic wavefunctions shown in the slides include the 1s state and the 2s / 2p states. They illustrate how n controls the radial distribution while l and mₗ determine angular behaviour. Their full algebraic forms are usually less useful for routine NEET numericals than the quantum-number, node and probability rules below.

1s: ψ₁₀₀ = (1/√π)(Z/a₀)3/2 e−Zr/a₀
2s: ψ₂₀₀ = [1/(4√(2π))](Z/a₀)3/2(2 − Zr/a₀)e−Zr/(2a₀)
2p, mₗ = 0: ψ₂₁₀ = [1/(4√(2π))](Z/a₀)3/2(Zr/a₀)e−Zr/(2a₀) cosθ
These are reference forms for hydrogen-like orbitals; the 2p expression carries angular dependence. Focus first on interpreting ψ and |ψ|², rather than memorising these full expressions.

Shell capacity

Orbitals in shell n = n²
Maximum electrons in shell n = 2n²
K shell (n=1): 1 orbital / 2 electrons; L (n=2): 4 orbitals / 8 electrons; M (n=3): 9 orbitals / 18 electrons; N (n=4): 16 orbitals / 32 electrons.

In a hydrogen atom, orbitals with the same n have the same energy in the simple model. In multi-electron atoms, shielding and penetration cause energy differences between subshells; use the Aufbau (n+l) rule for the usual filling sequence.

A node is a region where the wavefunction ψ, and therefore probability density |ψ|², is zero. Total nodes depend on n; angular nodes depend on l.

p-orbital nodal planes

pₓ has a yz nodal plane; pᵧ has an xz nodal plane; p_z has an xy nodal plane. These correspond to one angular node.

d-orbital orientation

dₓᵧ, dₓz and dᵧz have lobes between the corresponding axes; dₓ²₋ᵧ² has lobes along x and y; d_z² has two z-axis lobes plus a torus.

Radial probability and the Bohr radius

The radial distribution asks how likely the electron is to be found at a distance r from the nucleus, irrespective of direction. The 1s radial probability distribution of hydrogen is largest at the Bohr radius, a₀ = 0.529 Å. Higher s orbitals show radial nodes; p and d orbitals additionally have angular nodes.

Fast check for 3p: total = 2, angular = 1, radial = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1. For 3d: total = 2, angular = 2, radial = 0.
Common trap: the number of orbitals in a subshell is 2l+1, not 2(2l+1). The extra factor of 2 gives electron capacity because each orbital can hold two electrons.
12

Electronic configuration & filling rules

Aufbau, Pauli, Hund, energy order and important exceptions

Three fundamental rules

Aufbau principle

Electrons occupy lower-energy orbitals before higher-energy orbitals.

Pauli exclusion principle

No two electrons in an atom can have all four quantum numbers identical. One orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins.

Hund’s rule

Degenerate orbitals are singly occupied with parallel spins before electrons pair up.

Aufbau (n + l) rule

The subshell with lower n+l generally has lower energy. If two subshells have the same n+l value, the one with the lower n fills first.

Common filling sequence:
1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

Configurations worth memorising

SpeciesElectronic configurationWhy it matters

C1s² 2s² 2p²Hund’s rule in p orbitals

O1s² 2s² 2p⁴Pairing begins in 2p

Cr and Cu do not follow the simplest expected 4s² pattern. The close energies of 3d and 4s make the half-filled d⁵ and filled d¹⁰ arrangements particularly stable.

Transition-metal cations

For transition metals, remove the outer ns electrons before removing (n−1)d electrons. For example, Fe = [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²; Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶; Fe³⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵.

Half-filled / fully filled stability

The lecture notes give two explanations for the added stability often associated with exactly half-filled and fully filled subshells: symmetrical distribution of electrons and maximum exchange energy. This helps explain common configurations such as Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

Magnetic behaviour (from electron pairing)

  • Paramagnetic: at least one unpaired electron; attracted to a magnetic field.
  • Diamagnetic: all electrons paired; weakly repelled by a magnetic field; net spin-only magnetic moment is zero.
  • In p subshell filling: p¹ has 1 unpaired, p² has 2, p³ has 3, p⁴ has 2, p⁵ has 1 and p⁶ has 0.
Spin-only magnetic moment: μ = √[n(n + 2)] BM
n is the number of unpaired electrons; BM means Bohr magneton. For n = 0, μ = 0.

Draw orbital boxes and arrows whenever Hund’s rule or number of unpaired electrons is asked. Pairing begins in p with the 4th electron, in d with the 6th, and in f with the 8th electron.

Common trap: 4s generally fills before 3d in the neutral-atom Aufbau sequence, but 4s electrons are removed first when forming many transition-metal cations.
